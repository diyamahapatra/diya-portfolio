01. Overview

Problem

Students had to understand how UC Berkeley's advising is structured before they could get help from it.

Advising options sat in dropdown sections students opened one at a time. The difference between College and Major/Minor advisers was never stated. Requirements that determine whether a request can even proceed were buried near the bottom of the page.

Solution

Three pages, rebuilt around how students actually arrive.

A research-led redesign of three advising pages and a routing flow, built for a platform that couldn't support a single interactive element. Rather than explaining the college's structure more clearly, the redesign removes the need for students to understand it.

Appointments & Advice

Advising options grouped under labeled sections, with a side-by-side comparison of College and Major/Minor advising and a table surfacing the requirements students used to miss.

Appointments & Advice

Virtual Front Desk

Two drop-in services that used the same form and the same Zoom link, merged into one and compared against the choice students can actually make: a quick question now, or a scheduled appointment.

Virtual Front Desk

Forms & Petitions

Two concepts reorganizing forms around student situations rather than form types, with each entry labeled by who it's for, what format it is, and how to submit it. Scoped and designed, then set aside before testing so routing could get the attention it needed.

Routing Flow

A guided flow that asks students about their own situation and points them to the right adviser, form, or campus office.

02. Context

Context

The advising site is where students go when something has gone wrong.

L&S is Berkeley's largest college. Its advising site is where students go to book appointments, check deadlines, find petitions, and reach an adviser (usually at the point where something is already time-sensitive). The team was migrating from Drupal 7 to Drupal 10 and wanted to evaluate the experience before rebuilding it.

The Starting Point

261 survey responses, a migration deadline, and no assigned pages.

The team had collected student feedback but had not analyzed it. There was no brief naming which pages to redesign and the site was large and served many different needs, so finding where design could matter most was part of the work.

03. Research

Research

Making sense of 261 student voices

Five open-ended questions across 261 responses was more than I could hand-code in a twelve-week internship, so I used AskAI in Google Sheets for first-pass thematic coding. My first version forced one theme per response, which meant a comment naming two problems was counted as naming one. I rewrote the formulas to allow multiple themes per row and re-ran the set. Total cost: $0.34.

Thematic coding

Where students actually got lost

Survey insights told me students were frustrated but not where. I pulled Google Analytics to see what they were actually doing: 101,353 sessions across 46,473 users, with Appointments & Advice the single most-visited page at 8,884 sessions. Cross-referencing what students said against where they went pointed at three areas: Appointments & Advice, Virtual Front Desk, and Forms & Petitions. I presented that prioritization to the clients and they agreed.

The brief had initially asked for a heuristic evaluation. The research pointed at problems it couldn't solve because the issues lived across whole journeys, not in isolated interface flaws, so I mapped the end-to-end journeys instead.

Ideation

Four directions

I started with exploring four ways to reduce how much students needed to understand Berkeley’s advising structure for the advice and appointments page before finding the right service. Three concepts used guided questions to route students, while one focused on making the existing information easier to scan.

Then the platform changed the direction. Open Berkeley couldn't support the routing logic used by three of the four concepts, so I had to rethink how much of the experience could live directly on the page, which changed the trajectory of the rest of the focus pages.

04. Design Iterations

Build

What survived

The version that made it into Open Berkeley isn't the one I designed in Figma. The single “Get Advising” call to action became a link under L&S College Advising, and the services came back onto the page alongside it — now under labeled sections instead of dropdowns. What stayed was the principle: no student should have to infer a distinction the page could just state. The College vs. Major/Minor comparison became its own table. Special considerations became a three-column table of who's affected, what applies, and what to do first.

Build

A different kind of no

The second focus point was the Virtual Front Desk page. It asked students to choose between two services. Through research I had found out that they both use the same intake form and the same Zoom link, so I merged them into one routing form to kill the confusion — only internal staff needed to know the difference. I also replaced the giant Venn diagram with a table students could act on.

Annotations

Build

The routing had to live somewhere

If the page couldn't ask students anything, the questions had to live outside it. I prototyped the flow in Qualtrics, which handles multi-select and multi-step branching — a student could indicate several things at once and be routed on the combination.

The clients asked for Calendly instead, since most of the advising platform already ran on it. Calendly routes only on single-select questions, and each route resolves to exactly one destination, so the flow had to be flattened to one question at a time. I rebuilt it in Figma to make it testable. Forms & Petitions came off the plan to make room for this.

Prototyping

Making it real enough to test

Testing the design meant testing the interaction, not screenshots of it. The forms list had to remember both which tab was open and which section was expanded — 36 variants. Each drop-in calendar needed 10 states. I drove the Figma API through Claude Code to generate the component sets and wire the variant logic, which made it feasible at all.

It also produced the hardest bug of the project. The prototype lagged a full click behind, so clicking one accordion appeared to open the previous one. I verified all 72 interactions three times before finding the cause was an animation setting, not my wiring.

05. Testing

Method

Nine students, one week

I wrote the discussion guide and ran nine moderated usability sessions over a week in August. Participants were given a scenario and asked to show how they would resolve it rather than being told what to click, then shown the current pages for comparison. Sessions ran remotely over Zoom, with screen control passed to the participant for each task. Eleven were scheduled and two did not show.

Recruitment went out to roughly 23,000 students through an interest email pointing to a short screener, which collected class standing and how often students used the advising site. That let me select across class years and usage levels rather than taking whoever replied first. Participants ranged from incoming first-years to fifth-years and included transfer and out-of-state students.

Analysis

From recordings to recommendations

I transcribed and tagged the sessions in Dovetail, reviewing its AI-generated tags against the recordings and correcting them. Tagging by page and theme let me count how often each issue appeared and pull verbatim quotes behind every recommendation.

The redesign was preferred consistently. Removing the dropdowns drew the most praise, and seven of nine participants referenced the comparison tables positively.

The routing flow was where it broke. Seven of eight participants who reached a redirect looked at the calendar before the message telling them to go elsewhere. One concluded the page was broken. That traces back to the Qualtrics-to-Calendly switch: Calendly renders a scheduling interface even on routes that never end in a booking.

AI Workflow

Reliable at volume, unreliable at meaning

AskAI coded 261 responses in an afternoon, but my first pass forced one theme per response. Claude Code generated component sets I couldn't have built by hand, and also caused a bug that cost a full debugging session. Dovetail tagged nine sessions and read a statement of clarity as confusion.

Every failure was the same kind: fast and accurate at volume, unreliable the moment meaning was involved.

Limitations

What this study can't tell you

Recruitment

Prior users only

The screener selected for students who had used the site before, so first-time users are underrepresented.

Routing flow

One path tested

Participants were directed into the flow and all walked the same route, so I have no evidence about any other outcome.

Analysis

A single coder

Tagging and synthesis were done by one researcher with no second coder to check against.

06. Reflection

This project taught me to separate the problem I’m trying to solve from the solution I’m attached to. The experience changed several times as I learned more about Open Berkeley, stakeholder needs, and Calendly’s limitations, but the core problem stayed the same: students shouldn’t need to understand Berkeley’s advising structure just to know where to go.

I’d also bring implementation constraints into the process earlier. I still think it was valuable to explore beyond what the platform could support, but understanding those boundaries sooner would have helped me spend more time exploring solutions that could realistically ship.
